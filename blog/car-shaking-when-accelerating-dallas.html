Car Shaking When Accelerating? 10 Causes Dallas Drivers Should Know

November 25, 2026  ·  By Rico & Sam, CarNation Elite  ·  14 min read

Quick answer

When a car shakes while accelerating, the cause is usually a worn part that carries engine power to the wheels: an engine mount, a misfiring cylinder, an inner CV joint, the driveshaft, or a tire or wheel problem. When the shake happens (engine speed, road speed, throttle or braking) is the best clue, but only an inspection confirms it.

  • Lurch or shudder pulling away: often engine mounts, an inner CV joint or U-joints
  • Stumbling with a check engine light: often a misfire
  • Grows with road speed, even off the gas: often tires, wheels or the driveshaft
  • Brief "rumble strip" shudder at light throttle: often the torque converter clutch
  • Flashing check engine light, violent shaking or loose steering: pull over safely and get help

Safety First: When to Pull Over

A new vibration is a warning, even a mild one. "Mild" doesn't mean safe to keep driving: some of the causes below, like a separating tire or a failing U-joint, can start small and fail quickly. Until the car is checked, take it easy and avoid highway speeds and hard acceleration.

Pull over safely and arrange help if you notice

  • Violent shaking, a wobble or thumping that gets worse
  • A flashing check engine light
  • Tire damage: a bulge, a flat or a thumping tire
  • Loose, wandering or pulling steering
  • A burning smell or smoke from a wheel (overheating brakes)
  • A loud clunk or bang, or the car stops pulling
  • A new shake right after tire or wheel work

Get it checked promptly if you notice

  • A shudder under throttle that fades when you let off
  • A new vibration at highway speed
  • A clunk when shifting into Drive or Reverse
  • A hum or growl that changes when you turn
  • A steady check engine light or rough idle

How to pull over: turn on your hazard lights, ease off the gas, slow down gradually and move to a safe spot away from traffic. Don't inspect tires or wheels next to moving traffic, and don't touch the wheels: brakes and hubs can be hot enough to burn. If you're in a dangerous spot, call 911. Otherwise call for 24/7 roadside assistance or a tow. If you're unsure whether the car is safe, don't drive it.

Technicians sort a shake by what it follows. That one detail rules out half the list:

Why Dallas Cars Develop Acceleration Shakes

Every car vibrates a little. What matters is a new shake that shows up or gets worse when you press the gas. Acceleration is when the engine, transmission, axles and suspension carry the most force, so a worn part that stays quiet while coasting often shows itself under throttle.

Dallas roads speed up that wear. Potholes and expansion joints on I-635 (LBJ), I-35E and US-75 can bend wheels and knock tires out of balance. Our clay soil shifts and cracks pavement, which is hard on suspension parts. Heat and age also break down the rubber in engine mounts, CV boots and bushings.

The 10 Most Common Causes

1 Worn or Damaged Engine Mounts

Engine and transmission mounts are rubber-and-metal (sometimes fluid-filled) blocks that hold the powertrain in place and absorb its vibration. Under acceleration the engine twists against its mounts. Healthy mounts limit that movement; a torn or collapsed mount lets the engine rock and send vibration into the body.

How it feels: a clunk or lurch when you shift into Drive or Reverse, a shudder pulling away or under hard throttle, and often more vibration at idle in gear.

Heat, oil leaks and age break mount rubber down over time. When one mount fails, the others carry more load, so we inspect all of them.

What to do

Book an inspection soon. A badly broken mount lets the engine move more than it should, which strains axles, hoses and wiring.

2 Engine Misfires or Ignition Problems

A misfire happens when a cylinder doesn't burn its fuel properly, so the engine loses a power pulse and runs unevenly. Misfires are most noticeable under load, which is why they often show up when you accelerate, climb a hill or merge.

How it feels: stumbling, jerking or bucking when you press the gas, loss of power and a rough idle. The check engine light may come on, sometimes only after the misfire repeats.

Common causes include worn spark plugs, a failing ignition coil, a clogged or leaking fuel injector, a vacuum leak, low fuel pressure and low compression. A trouble code may name the cylinder, but not the cause. That's the difference between a code read and a real diagnostic test.

What to do

A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire. Owner's manuals warn that it can quickly cause serious catalytic converter damage and loss of power (source). Ease off the throttle, pull over safely and arrange to have the car checked or towed rather than driving on. With a steady light, book an engine diagnostic promptly. See also 15 common check engine light causes.

3 Damaged or Worn CV Axles and Joints

Front-wheel-drive and all-wheel-drive vehicles, and many rear-drive cars with independent rear suspension, use constant-velocity (CV) axles to carry power to the wheels while the suspension moves and the wheels steer. Each axle has an inner and an outer joint, packed with grease and sealed by a rubber boot.

How it feels: a worn inner joint typically causes a side-to-side shudder under acceleration that fades when you let off. A worn outer joint usually clicks or pops in tight turns.

A torn boot flings grease out and lets dirt and water in, and the joint wears quickly after that. A torn boot caught early can sometimes be replaced on its own; once the joint is noisy or vibrating, the axle usually needs replacement.

What to do

Have it inspected soon. A badly worn joint can eventually come apart, leaving the car without drive to that wheel.

4 Driveshaft or Driveline Problems

Rear-wheel-drive cars, most trucks and many AWD SUVs use a driveshaft to send power to the rear axle. Worn U-joints, a failing center support bearing on two-piece shafts, a damaged or out-of-balance shaft, or changed driveline angles (for example after a lift kit or with sagging springs) can all cause vibration, especially under load.

How it feels: vibration through the floor or seat that builds with speed or throttle, a clunk when you take off or shift, or a squeak that rises with speed.

What to do

Don't put it off. A failed U-joint can let the driveshaft separate, which can damage the vehicle and affect control. If you hear a loud clunk or banging under the car, pull over safely and arrange a tow. Running work trucks? See our fleet services.

5 Tire Imbalance, Cupping, or Separation

Tires are one of the most common sources of vibration. An out-of-balance tire usually shakes the steering wheel (front) or the seat (rear) in a particular speed range, often highway speeds, whether you're accelerating or coasting. Cupping, a scalloped wear pattern, usually points to worn shocks or struts, poor alignment or long-term imbalance.

How it feels: a speed-related vibration, plus road noise from cupped tires. Tire separation feels different: a thumping wobble, even at low speed, that gets worse.

A hard pothole or curb hit can damage a tire's internal structure. When the car is parked safely, look over each tire for bulges, cuts and uneven wear. Safety agencies list bulges, cuts and cracks in the sidewall as problems that can lead to tire failure (source).

What to do

A bulge, a thumping wobble or a tire losing air means don't keep driving on it. Pull over safely; change to the spare only if you can do it well away from traffic, or call roadside assistance. For a speed-related vibration, a tire inspection and rebalance is usually the first step.

6 Bent Wheels, Rim Damage, or Loose Lug Nuts

A wheel doesn't need visible damage to be bent. A hard pothole or curb hit can put a slight bend in the inner lip that balancing weights can't fully cancel out. Low-profile tires on large wheels are especially vulnerable.

How it feels: a vibration that comes back after balancing, or a slow leak from the same tire. A wobble or clunking that starts right after tire or wheel work can mean loose lug nuts: pull over safely and don't drive on it.

What to do

Have the wheel checked for runout (wobble) and cracks. Many vehicle manufacturers don't approve straightening, welding or reshaping wheels, because it can weaken them; they allow only cosmetic refinishing and recommend replacing bent or cracked wheels (sources). Replace a damaged wheel with one that matches the original specifications.

7 Worn Suspension Parts or Control Arm Bushings

Control arm bushings are rubber pivots that locate each wheel. When you accelerate, the tires push the car forward and pull back on the control arms; on front-wheel-drive cars, steering parts also carry drive torque. Worn bushings, ball joints or tie rod ends let the wheel shift and its alignment change under that load.

How it feels: steering that tugs or wanders when you accelerate, a shimmy under throttle, clunks over bumps and uneven tire wear. Worn shocks and struts mainly cause bouncing and cupped tires rather than an acceleration shake.

Cracked, uneven Dallas streets put steady stress on bushings and ball joints. A suspension check is worth doing whenever you replace tires.

What to do

If the steering feels loose or the car wanders, pull over safely and have it towed; steering and suspension parts affect control in an emergency. Otherwise book an inspection soon. See steering and suspension repair in Dallas.

8 Transmission Issues or Torque Converter Problems

In most automatic transmissions, the torque converter has a clutch that locks the engine to the transmission to save fuel. On many modern vehicles it applies, often with a small amount of controlled slip, at light throttle across a wide speed range. If the clutch or its fluid is worn, it can grab and slip as it applies.

How it feels: a brief shudder at light throttle and steady speed, often described as driving over rumble strips. Manufacturer bulletins describe it anywhere from about 20 to 80 mph depending on the vehicle (sources). Other transmission signs include slipping (RPM rises without the car speeding up), harsh or delayed shifts and a warning light. CVTs and dual-clutch transmissions can also judder, usually when pulling away.

Shudder is often caused by worn or contaminated fluid rather than a failed converter. Manufacturer fixes range from a specific fluid exchange procedure to a software update, and a converter is replaced only when testing confirms it. A misfire can feel similar, so we check engine and transmission data together before recommending anything.

What to do

Book a diagnosis before it gets worse. A slipping transmission builds heat and debris that can lead to a bigger repair. See transmission repair in Dallas.

9 Sticking Brake Caliper or Warped Rotors

Uneven rotors (often called warped) mostly cause vibration when you brake, not when you accelerate. If your steering wheel or pedal pulses as you stop, start with our guide to brake noises and what they mean.

The brake problem that can show up while accelerating and cruising is a sticking (binding) caliper or a dragging parking brake. The pad keeps rubbing the rotor, building heat, making the car feel sluggish and sometimes adding vibration.

How it feels: the car pulls to one side and feels like the brakes are partly on, with a hot or burning smell. Don't touch the wheel to check: it can be hot enough to burn.

What to do

If you smell burning or see smoke from a wheel, pull over safely, let the brakes cool and don't drive on; call for help. Overheated brakes can lose stopping power. See brake repair in Dallas.

10 Bad Wheel Bearings

Wheel bearings let each wheel spin smoothly on its hub. As a bearing wears, it develops roughness and play, which you usually hear before you feel.

How it feels: a hum, growl or grinding noise that gets louder with speed and often changes when you turn or change lanes, plus vibration in the floor or steering wheel as it worsens. Some cars also turn on the ABS light, because the wheel speed sensor is part of the hub.

Pothole and curb impacts can damage bearings, and so can water from flooded streets getting past the seals.

What to do

Book an inspection soon. If the noise turns to loud grinding or the wheel feels loose, pull over safely; a badly worn bearing can overheat and, in severe cases, let the wheel wobble or seize.

How We Diagnose a Shake at Our Floyd Circle Shop

Guessing at vibration problems gets expensive, because several of these parts feel the same from the driver's seat. Here's how we narrow it down at 13610 Floyd Circle:

  1. Your description. When does it shake, at what speed, on or off the gas? Any recent pothole hit or tire work?
  2. Safety check first. Before anyone drives the car, we look at the tires, wheels and lug nuts, steering and suspension parts, brakes and leaks. If we find something unsafe, we don't road-test the car until it's fixed and we'll explain why.
  3. Scan data. A full scan and live data, such as misfire counters, fuel trims and torque converter slip, separates an engine problem from a transmission one.
  4. Road test, when it's safe. On suitable roads we reproduce the shake and check whether it follows engine speed, road speed or throttle.
  5. Written estimate. You get a clear explanation of what we found, what's urgent and what can wait, before any repair starts.

Some problems, such as module or software faults, need more than a scan. Engine and transmission computer (ECM/PCM) diagnostics are a specialty at our shop. You'll know the diagnostic price before we start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my car shake when I accelerate but not when I coast?

A shake that appears under throttle and fades when you let off usually points to a part that only carries load when the engine is pushing the car, such as a worn inner CV joint, a broken engine or transmission mount, worn control arm bushings or a U-joint. A misfire under load can feel similar. An inspection is needed to confirm which one it is.

Why does my car shake at highway speeds?

A vibration that builds with road speed and stays there whether you're on the gas or coasting is most often a tire balance problem, tire damage or a bent wheel, and on rear-wheel-drive vehicles sometimes the driveshaft. A tire and wheel inspection is usually the first step. If the shaking is severe or sudden, pull over safely.

Is it safe to drive a car that shakes when accelerating?

Not necessarily, even if the shaking is mild, because some causes start small and fail quickly. Pull over safely and arrange roadside help or a tow if you have severe shaking, a flashing check engine light, tire damage, loose or pulling steering, a burning smell from a wheel or a loud clunk. For anything else, avoid highway speeds and hard acceleration and get the car checked promptly.

Can a bad spark plug cause shaking when accelerating?

Yes. A worn spark plug or failing ignition coil can cause a misfire, which feels like stumbling or jerking under acceleration and may turn on the check engine light. A flashing check engine light means a severe misfire that can quickly damage the catalytic converter, so ease off, pull over safely and have the car checked.

Can bad engine mounts cause shaking when accelerating?

Yes. Worn or broken engine and transmission mounts let the engine rock under torque, which can cause a shudder or lurch when you accelerate or shift into Drive or Reverse, and often more vibration at idle.
